# The Church Machine — for Everyone

**What it feels like to use. No jargon, no keys, no set-up.**

---

## The whole thing in three words

**Write. Use. Deploy.**

You write a program. You use a machine. You deploy the program to the
machine. That is the entire experience. Everything else — and there is a great
deal else — happens underneath, where you never have to look.

---

## 1 — Write

You type a program and press Compile. It gets a name you choose, like
`led.blink` or `Mother`.

That is all you do. Behind the name, the machine has given your program a
permanent fingerprint, so that the same program always has the same identity
and there is never a version number to manage. Write it twice, it is the same
thing twice. Change it, and it is a new thing — the old one is still there,
still safe, still runnable.

You never see the fingerprint unless you go looking. You just see the name.

---

## 2 — Use a machine

Plug in a Church Machine and turn it on. Open the app.

The app says: **"New machine found. Use it?"**

You click yes. That is the whole set-up. The machine is now yours and the app
knows it. No account to make, no code to type, no key to keep. If you plug in
five machines, each one says "found — use it?" and you click yes five times.

It is as simple as connecting a pair of wireless headphones. It just works,
and the reasons it is safe are not your problem.

---

## 3 — Deploy

You have a program (`Mother`) and a machine (`CM-3`). You pick the machine and
press Deploy.

The app says: **"Mother is running on CM-3."**

Done. Your program is now alive on that machine. You can deploy it to as many
machines as you like — pick each one, press Deploy. Each machine runs its own
copy, and each copy is sealed to that machine. That sealing is automatic and
invisible; you never arrange it.

---

## What you see, all of it

A list of your machines. Each one shows a plain status:

- **Ready** — powered, yours, waiting for something to run.
- **Running Mother** — busy, doing its job.
- **Belongs to someone else** — you plugged in a machine that is still tied to
  its previous owner. (See below.)
- **Stopped responding** — a machine that was yours has gone quiet.

That is the entire surface. Names, machines, statuses. Nothing to configure.

---

## The three uncommon moments

Most days you only Write, Use, and Deploy. Occasionally one of these happens,
and each is a single plain instruction — never a puzzle.

### "This machine belongs to someone else"

You got a machine second-hand and it is still tied to whoever had it before.
The app tells you plainly, and tells you the fix: **ask the seller to release
it.** Once they do — a single action on their side, often just handing you a
code to scan — the machine becomes yours and you Use it normally.

This is not a hurdle; it is a protection. It is the reason a stolen machine is
useless to a thief: it stays tied to its owner until the owner lets go.

### "I want to start this machine over"

Maybe you are repurposing a machine, or you bought it used and want a clean
slate. **Hold the reset button while you power it on.** The machine wipes
itself and comes back brand new — ready for you to Use it fresh.

That is the whole procedure. Hold a button, power on. Like resetting any other
device. What it quietly guarantees is that "starting over" is a true fresh
start: whatever ran there before is genuinely gone, not hiding.

### "A machine stopped responding"

If a machine that was yours goes quiet, the app tells you: **"CM-3 stopped
responding and may have been reset."** That is your early warning — if you did
not reset it yourself, someone else may have taken it. You will know, because
an honest machine keeps checking in, and a taken one cannot pretend to be the
one you knew.

## The one optional nicety: your ID as a QR code

By default you never think about identity. The app quietly is "you," and your
machines follow this app.

But if you want your machines to follow *you* instead of *this one app on this
one laptop*, you can save your ID as a QR code. Then:

- **New laptop?** Scan your QR on the new one, and all your machines recognise
  you again. Nothing lost.
- **Selling a machine?** Hand the buyer a QR code. They scan it, the machine is
  theirs. Done.
- **Sharing with family or a team?** Everyone scans the same ID, and you all
  share the machines.

You never *have* to do this. The app works completely without it. It is there
for people who want their machines tied to them across devices — and it turns
the awkward moments (new laptop, selling, sharing) into a single scan.

## Why you can trust it without understanding it

Here is the only promise you need, and you can take it on faith or read the
expert document for the proof:

**A program on a Church Machine can only touch what it was given.** Not what it
can find, not what it can reach, not what it can trick its way into — only what
it was explicitly handed. There is no back door, because there is no "back" to
go to. This is true of every program, including a powerful one, including an
AI. It lives inside exactly the authority you granted it, and there is no
instruction in the machine that lets it exceed that.

You do not arrange this. You cannot switch it off. It is how the machine is
built, the way a sealed room is not a room with a very good lock but a room
with no door in the wall you are worried about. That is why using it is safe
and simple at the same time: the safety is in the walls, not in anything you
have to do.

## What about super-smart AI and quantum computers?

Two fears are worth answering plainly, because you have probably heard that
they break everything. They do not break this — and the reasons are worth
knowing even if you never look under the hood.

### Super-smart AI

The danger of a very clever attacker — human or artificial — is that it finds
the way in that you never thought of. The overlooked door. The clever trick.
The path through the wall you did not know was there.

Most security tries to win that contest by being cleverer than the attacker.
That is a race, and against something smarter than you, it is a race you lose.

The Church Machine does not race. It removes the doors. A program here can only
touch what it was handed — and no amount of cleverness finds a door in a wall
that has none. A brilliant program and a stupid program are contained by the
very same walls, and being brilliant buys no way through, because the way
through was never a matter of brains. It was a matter of doors, and there are
none.

So a very powerful AI, running on a Church Machine, is held by exactly the same
thing that holds the smallest program: it lives inside the authority it was
given, and its intelligence gains it nothing beyond that. This is perhaps the
most important thing the machine does. It does not try to outsmart what it
contains. It makes outsmarting beside the point.

### Quantum computers

Quantum computers are astonishing, but they do not break locks in general —
they break *particular kinds of mathematics*. And the lock at the heart of the
Church Machine — the thing that makes a program's authority unforgeable — does
not use the kind of mathematics a quantum computer breaks. It is built on a
secret the machine keeps inside itself and never shows anyone, and you cannot
compute your way to a secret you are not allowed to see.

There is one part of the system — the signature that proves an instruction came
from you and not an impostor — that a future quantum computer could eventually
counterfeit. But that part is a single, replaceable component, and the
replacements already exist: newer signature methods, built specifically to
resist quantum computers, standardised and ready. Swapping to them changes
nothing about how you use the machine and nothing about how it contains
programs. It is a part we can upgrade before quantum computers are a practical
threat, the way you would replace one lock on a door without rebuilding the
house.

### The honest part

Here is the one thing worth being straight about. All of this — the walls, the
sealed room, the containment — is only as strong as the physical machine that
enforces it. The *design* is sound against clever AI and ready for quantum
computers. Making the *hardware* enforce it perfectly, even against someone who
physically attacks the chip itself, is real work still underway. A determined,
powerful adversary would aim there — at the silicon — because that is where the
promises are still being made solid.

So the honest answer is: the thinking is right, the mathematics is ready or
readily made ready, and the remaining battle is in the hardware — which is
exactly where the machine's builders are working now. The walls are designed
correctly. The work that remains is making them, in the physical world, as
unbreakable as they are on paper.
